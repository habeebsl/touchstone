# What Touchstone claims

The technical claim stated properly, what it rests on, and honest accounting of which parts of it
are ours and which are Perfect Corp's. Companion to [RESEARCH.md](RESEARCH.md), which holds the
user and market evidence.

---

## 1. What we claim, and what we don't

**We do not claim** to read skin better than Perfect Corp. We use `skin-tone-analysis` and
`fitzpatrick-scale-analyzer` for exactly that. Claiming to beat them at their own measurement is
false and trivially checked.

**We claim** two things, and the second is the one that matters:

1. Every shade clears a measured perceptual-visibility floor against that person's own skin and
   lip colour, verified across the full Fitzpatrick range.
2. Colour is *placed* for measured depth rather than by the conventional rule, which is a
   fair-skin assumption that collapses to near-black at the deep end. See §2 — this is the half
   that does the work, and the first version of this document had it the wrong way round.

The accuracy story is **correct by construction, not better sensors.**

## 2. The technical claim, stated properly

### It is the placement, not the guard

The guard pushes a shade away from the skin until it clears a perceptual floor. Measured across
the fixtures, it fires on **fair** colouring — blush and eyeshadow both get pushed on the fairest
fixture — and on the deepest fixture every shade clears its floor untouched. It is a backstop, and
it is not what makes this work on deep skin.

What does is where lightness is placed, before the guard runs. Makeup colour is conventionally
placed below the skin's own lightness. That is a fair-skin assumption: there is room below fair
skin and almost none below deep skin, where what room exists is the region sRGB cannot hold a
saturated colour in at all. So the conventional rule does not produce a deeper shade there — it
produces something close to black.

Generated by `npm run sweep`, one look across the range:

| Colouring | Fitz | Placed the usual way | Placed for depth | ΔE moved | Looks affected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Very fair, cool | I | `#f83698` (chroma 0.238) | `#f83698` (chroma 0.238) | 0.000 | 0/5 |
| Light, cool | II | `#e31e85` (chroma 0.234) | `#e31e85` (chroma 0.234) | 0.000 | 0/5 |
| Medium, warm | IV | `#80341e` (chroma 0.111) | `#80341e` (chroma 0.111) | 0.000 | 0/5 |
| Olive, neutral | IV | `#7c301a` (chroma 0.111) | `#7c301a` (chroma 0.111) | 0.000 | 0/5 |
| Deep, warm | V | `#461a0c` (chroma 0.072) | `#5d1801` (chroma 0.105) | 0.052 | 5/5 |
| Deepest | VI | `#050403` (chroma 0.005) | `#a7075d` (chroma 0.191) | 0.414 | 5/5 |

Two things to read off it. **The rule does nothing until it is needed** — identical on the five
lighter fixtures, so it is not a special case bolted on for deep skin. And **the guard cannot
rescue it**: that "usual way" column is the shade *after* the guard has run and tried to push it
clear.

### Distance is not visibility

The trap, and the sharpest point available to us. On the deepest fixture the conventional
placement scores ΔE **0.247** from her skin and the adapted one scores **0.205** — so a pure
distance test passes the black and ranks it *higher*. Black is a long way from skin and still not
a lipstick. A shade has to clear a distance **and** survive as a colour, which is chroma, and
`placement.check.ts` asserts both.

### And it is still constraint satisfaction

Four constraints hold simultaneously:

1. Far enough from her **skin** that it reads as makeup
2. Far enough from her **own lip colour** that you can tell something was applied
3. Still inside her **palette** — hue clamped to ±16°
4. **Distinct from the other four looks**, or the set stops being a choice

They fight. On 13 Aug, scaling the natural-lip gap by application intensity satisfied 2 and
immediately broke 4 — `Polished and Feline share a lip colour`, `bold no more striking than soft`.
It was reverted. The check suite caught a plausible change collapsing the product, which is the
evidence that this is constraint satisfaction rather than a threshold.

### Shown, not asserted

The product carries its own working. `pickColour` records where it placed a shade against where
the conventional rule would have put it; a look carries both, plus every visibility check that
ran. `PlacementProof` shows the pair, and on request renders the same look with that one rule
switched off — one API unit — so the comparison is two faces rather than two hex values.

The counterfactual is derived, not staged: same look, same accent, same guards, one rule off, and
only the lip colour substituted into the effects. It is labelled *placed the usual way* and never
as anyone else's product, because it is our own engine with a rule disabled and claiming otherwise
would not survive scrutiny.

## 3. Where the APIs are load-bearing

Honest accounting, because a judge will ask.

| API | Role | Load-bearing? |
|---|---|---|
| `skin-tone-analysis` | Measured skin, lip, hair, eye colour | **Yes** — the guarantee is measured against these. Without real values there is nothing to compute visibility against |
| `fitzpatrick-scale-analyzer` | Independent depth reading | **Yes** — cross-checks the measurement, drives register selection |
| `makeup-vto` | Renders the finished look | **Yes** — the output device for the whole pipeline |
| `sod` | Background removal before garment palette extraction | **Yes**, and off-label — it is a background remover used as colour-extraction preprocessing |

The visibility guard itself is arithmetic on hex values; no API does that part, and we should not
imply otherwise. The framing that is both true and generous to the sponsor:

> Their measurement makes the guarantee possible. The guarantee is what makes the measurement
> useful.
